# Scientific References

This page collects real, peer-reviewed studies related to the perceptual
concepts referenced in [Optic flow and kinesthetic
movement](02-optic-flow-and-kinesthetics.md) — optic flow, vection, and
low-frequency (rocking/"heave") vestibular stimulation — and to the
"blue mind" observation referenced in [Background and
purpose](01-background-and-purpose.md). Citations use Harvard style, with
a PubMed/PMC or publisher DOI link for each.

> **Note on scope:** No study listed here was conducted on Aquafulness, and
> none tests a floating ocean visualization specifically. They establish the
> component perceptual and physiological effects — that coherent optic
> flow and vection can be generated visually, that slow rocking/vestibular
> stimulation is associated with relaxation and sleep onset, and that
> looking at water and nature scenes is associated with stress recovery —
> that the design combines by analogy. As the disclaimers elsewhere in this
> folder state, this is design reasoning grounded in existing literature and
> personal experience, not a clinical claim about Aquafulness itself.

## Optic flow, vection and self-motion perception

- Wright, W.G., DiZio, P. and Lackner, J.R. (2005) 'Vertical linear
  self-motion perception during visual and inertial motion: more than
  weighted summation of sensory inputs', *Journal of Vestibular Research*,
  15(4), pp. 185–195. PubMed:
  [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16286700/](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16286700/)

  Shows that oscillatory *vertical* visual motion alone — without any
  physical movement — induces the perception of vertical self-oscillation,
  the basic mechanism behind the camera-bob technique.

- Nakamura, S., Palmisano, S. and Kim, J. (2016) 'Relative visual
  oscillation can facilitate visually induced self-motion perception',
  *i-Perception*, 7(4), pp. 1–18. doi:
  [10.1177/2041669516661903](https://doi.org/10.1177/2041669516661903).
  PubMed: [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27698982/](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27698982/)

- Wang, W., Yang, K. and Zhu, Y. (2021) 'Optimal frequency and amplitude of
  vertical viewpoint oscillation for improving vection strength and
  reducing neural constraints on gait', *Entropy*, 23(5), 541. doi:
  [10.3390/e23050541](https://doi.org/10.3390/e23050541). PMC:
  [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8145014/](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8145014/)

  Directly studies vertical viewpoint oscillation (the "whole-field
  rocking" layer) and reports frequency/amplitude combinations that
  strengthen vection — relevant to tuning the app's camera-bob parameters.

- Nooij, S.A.E., Pretto, P. and Bülthoff, H.H. (2018) 'More vection means
  more velocity storage activity: a factor in visually induced motion
  sickness?', *Experimental Brain Research*, 236(11), pp. 3031–3041. doi:
  [10.1007/s00221-018-5340-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00221-018-5340-1).
  PubMed: [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30120498/](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30120498/)

  A caveat reference: stronger vection is also linked to a higher risk of
  visually induced motion sickness, which is why the app keeps motion slow
  and user-adjustable rather than maximizing vection strength.

## Visual flow and autonomic (vagal/parasympathetic) response

- Yang, A.H.X., Khwaounjoo, P. and Cakmak, Y.O. (2021) 'Directional effects
  of whole-body spinning and visual flow in virtual reality on vagal
  neuromodulation', *Journal of Vestibular Research*, 31(6), pp. 479–494.
  doi: [10.3233/VES-201574](https://doi.org/10.3233/VES-201574). PubMed:
  [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34024797/](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34024797/)

- Kuldavletova, O., Denise, P., Normand, H., Quarck, G. and Etard, O.M.
  (2023) 'Both whole-body rotation and visual flow induce cardiovascular
  autonomic response in human, but visual response is overridden by
  vestibular stimulation', *Scientific Reports*, 13, 4191. doi:
  [10.1038/s41598-023-31431-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-31431-z).
  PMC: [https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10015060/](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10015060/)

  Both studies find that optic flow alone — with no physical motion —
  measurably shifts vagal/parasympathetic activity, the physiological
  substrate of the "calming" claim made about coherent visual flow.

## Rocking, vestibular ("heave") stimulation, sleep and relaxation

- Bayer, L., Constantinescu, I., Perrig, S., Vienne, J., Vidal, P.-P.,
  Mühlethaler, M. and Schwartz, S. (2011) 'Rocking synchronizes brain waves
  during a short nap', *Current Biology*, 21(12), pp. R461–R462. doi:
  [10.1016/j.cub.2011.05.012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2011.05.012).
  PubMed: [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21683897/](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21683897/)

- Omlin, X., Crivelli, F., Näf, M., Heinicke, L., Skorucak, J., Malafeev,
  A., Fernandez Guerrero, A., Riener, R. and Achermann, P. (2018) 'The
  effect of a slowly rocking bed on sleep', *Scientific Reports*, 8, 2156.
  doi: [10.1038/s41598-018-19880-3](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-018-19880-3).
  PubMed: [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29391413/](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29391413/)

- Perrault, A.A., Khani, A., Quairiaux, C., Kompotis, K., Franken, P.,
  Mühlethaler, M., Schwartz, S. and Bayer, L. (2019) 'Whole-night continuous
  rocking entrains spontaneous neural oscillations with benefits for sleep
  and memory', *Current Biology*, 29(3), pp. 402–411.e3. doi:
  [10.1016/j.cub.2018.12.028](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2018.12.028)

- van Sluijs, R.M., Rondei, Q.J., Schluep, D., Jäger, L., Riener, R.,
  Achermann, P. and Wilhelm, E. (2020) 'Effect of rocking movements on
  afternoon sleep', *Frontiers in Neuroscience*, 13, 1446. doi:
  [10.3389/fnins.2019.01446](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2019.01446).
  PubMed: [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32038144/](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32038144/)

- Subramaniam, A., Eberhard-Moscicka, A.K., Ertl, M. and Mast, F.W. (2023)
  'Rocking devices and the role of vestibular stimulation on sleep — a
  systematic review', *Clinical and Translational Neuroscience*, 7(4), 40.
  doi: [10.3390/ctn7040040](https://doi.org/10.3390/ctn7040040)

  These studies are the direct evidence base for the app's central
  analogy: slow (roughly 0.25 Hz), passive rocking — the same low-frequency
  vertical "heave" motion the camera applies to the whole visual field —
  measurably promotes relaxation, faster sleep onset and deeper sleep in
  humans, and is understood to work through rhythmic vestibular
  stimulation.

## Watching water and stress recovery ("blue mind")

- Ulrich, R.S., Simons, R.F., Losito, B.D., Fiorito, E., Miles, M.A. and
  Zelson, M. (1991) 'Stress recovery during exposure to natural and urban
  environments', *Journal of Environmental Psychology*, 11(3), pp.
  201–230. doi:
  [10.1016/S0272-4944(05)80184-7](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0272-4944(05)80184-7)

  The foundational study showing that video of natural scenes produces
  faster physiological stress recovery (heart rate, skin conductance,
  muscle tension) than video of urban scenes.

- White, M.P., Smith, A., Humphryes, K., Pahl, S., Snelling, D. and
  Depledge, M. (2010) 'Blue space: the importance of water for preference,
  affect, and restorativeness ratings of natural and built scenes',
  *Journal of Environmental Psychology*, 30(4), pp. 482–493. doi:
  [10.1016/j.jenvp.2010.04.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvp.2010.04.004)

  Found that scenes containing water are rated higher for preference,
  positive affect and perceived restorativeness than equivalent scenes
  without water — direct empirical grounding for the "blue mind"/watching-
  water effect referenced in the background document, independent of any
  motion illusion.
